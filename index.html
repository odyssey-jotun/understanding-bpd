Understanding Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)

Borderline personality disorder can affect emotions, relationships, self-image, and impulse control. Three free guides bring together research on BPD, evidence-based treatment, emotional regulation, and family support in one place.

Borderline Personality Disorder Can Be Difficult to Recognize

BPD does not look exactly the same in every person. Its diagnostic framework spans emotional regulation, relationships, identity, impulsivity, anger, self-harm, and stress-related changes in thinking.[1]

5 of 9

Diagnostic Criteria

A diagnosis requires at least five of nine DSM-5-TR criteria. Those criteria can involve abandonment fears, unstable relationships, identity disturbance, impulsivity, self-harm or suicidality, mood reactivity, emptiness, anger, and stress-related paranoia or dissociation.[1]

The breadth of the criteria helps explain why BPD can present very differently from one person to another.

5.9% vs. 1.4%

Suicide Risk

A comprehensive review reported death by suicide in 5.9% of people with BPD compared with 1.4% among people with other personality disorders.[2]

The same review describes substantial impairment in relationships and daily functioning, which is one reason early recognition and evidence-based care matter.

92% · 63% · 74%

Coping Skills Take Practice

In a qualitative study of 24 patients learning distress-tolerance skills, 92% reported difficulty engaging with the skills, 63% said finding the right skill was extremely difficult, and 74% described frustration when a skill did not work at first.[3]

Half also said the skills helped them stop ruminating and think clearly again, showing why practice and fit matter.

Marley’s research also examined rejection sensitivity, mentalizing, trauma, parenting, family burden, and the interaction between biological vulnerability and environment.[4][5]

Better Information Can Change the Conversation

Confusion can make difficult relationships harder. Without a framework for understanding emotional dysregulation, fear of abandonment, impulsivity, or rejection sensitivity, behavior can easily feel inexplicable or personal.

Understanding BPD does not mean excusing harmful behavior. It provides language for recognizing patterns, setting appropriate boundaries, communicating more effectively, and knowing when professional help may be necessary.

BPD Is Treatable

Psychotherapy is the primary treatment for BPD. Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT, was developed specifically for BPD and teaches four major skill areas.[6]

Mindfulness

Observing and participating in the present moment without immediately judging the experience.

Distress Tolerance

Getting through intense emotional moments without adding new problems to the crisis.

Emotion Regulation

Identifying emotions and triggers so strong reactions become easier to understand and manage.

Interpersonal Effectiveness

Expressing needs, setting boundaries, and protecting self-respect while preserving important relationships.

22 studies

Shorter DBT Programs Have Been Studied Too

A 2025 systematic review of 22 studies found brief DBT-informed interventions generally feasible and effective for reducing BPD-specific symptoms and improving broader psychiatric symptoms and coping skills. Evidence was more mixed for severe behavioral outcomes such as suicide attempts and self-harm.[7]

My Name Is Marley

My interest in BPD began inside my own family.

Growing up around emotional and behavioral patterns I did not understand left me with questions that became easier to articulate once I began learning about borderline personality disorder.

I decided to research those questions rather than leave them unanswered. I studied diagnostic criteria, emotional regulation, neurobiology, developmental and environmental factors, treatment approaches, and research involving families and caregivers.

I then turned that research into three guides. They are not diagnostic tools or substitutes for professional mental-health care. They are designed to make credible information easier to find and give people a clearer place to begin.

Families Can Learn Skills Too

121 family members were included in a recent randomized trial.

Family-focused research has found that psychoeducation and skills-based programs can reduce caregiver burden and improve knowledge, coping, and family functioning. A 2024 randomized controlled trial of Family Connections included 121 relatives and found stronger adjusted outcomes across several family measures.[8]
